The principle behind the whole system

Two independent kinds of evidence that agree are worth more than any amount of one kind.

YouTube performance and Google search demand are measured by different companies, from different behaviour, with different errors. A topic can fool one of them. It rarely fools both.

First principles

What actually makes
a video get views?

Strip it down and a video's views are the product of four things. Only one of them is the thing you are trying to choose.

views ≈ channel × topic × packaging × luck
FactorWhat it isCan you copy it?
ChannelSubscribers, watch history, how hard the algorithm already pushes this creator. It explains most of the raw number.No. A channel with 2M subscribers can get 200,000 views on an ordinary video.
TopicHow many people want a video about this, right now.Yes. This is the factor you are shopping for.
PackagingTitle, thumbnail, hook, quality of the video itself.Partly. It is your craft, not the topic's merit.
LuckTiming, a news event, one big share.No.

Seen this way, the usual ways of finding ideas each confuse the topic with another factor:

MethodWhat goes wrong
Copy the videos with the most viewsRaw views mostly measure the channel. You end up copying big creators' ordinary videos.
Copy one viral videoOne data point can't separate topic from packaging and luck. And you only see the winners: the hundred videos on the same topic that flopped are invisible.
Use a keyword tool aloneSearch volume proves people want an answer, not that they want a video, and not that videos on it outperform.
Trust your gutNo measurement at all, so no way to learn when it was wrong.

So the job is

Isolate the topic factor. Two moves do it.

Normalize: divide every video's views by what its own channel normally gets. That cancels the channel. Replicate: demand the same result from many unrelated channels. Packaging and luck differ from channel to channel, so they average out. What is left is the topic.

The YouTube tool  ·  foundation

One number drives
everything: the median.

Before any step runs, every channel the tool touches gets a baseline: the median views of its last 50 uploads, in one format. Every video is then judged against its own channel's baseline, never against a raw view count.

channel_median = median( views of the last UPLOADS_FOR_MEDIAN uploads, in FORMAT ) multiple = video_views ÷ channel_median

First principle: normalize

Dividing by the channel's median cancels the channel.

In views ≈ channel × topic × packaging × luck, the median is a direct estimate of the channel term: what this creator gets on an ordinary day. Divide by it and what remains is how much this particular video beat the ordinary. That is why 20,000 views on a channel that normally gets 2,000 (a 10x) is a stronger signal than 200,000 on a channel that normally gets 150,000 (a 1.3x).

Why the median and not the average

A channel's views are lopsided: many ordinary videos and an occasional monster. The average gets dragged toward the monster. The median doesn't move.

Illustration: nine uploads of one channel, on a log scale. Eight sit between 8,000 and 14,000 views. One got 410,000.

The average says a normal video here gets 55,222 views. Not one of the eight normal videos comes near that. The median says 11,000, which is exactly what a normal video gets. Measured against the median the viral video is a 37x. Measured against the average it would be a 7x, and worse: the viral video would be hiding inside its own baseline, making itself look less exceptional.

Two details that keep the baseline honest

  • Formats are never mixed. Shorts and long-form videos live on different view scales. A baseline that blends them describes neither, so FORMAT picks one and the other is ignored everywhere.
  • Only recent uploads count. The last 50 uploads describe the channel as it is now. A channel that grew 10x last year should not be judged against what it got before.

The YouTube tool  ·  steps 1 and 2

Find the outliers.
Then find the topics.

Step 1: outliers are hypotheses, not answers

The tool scans every channel in CHANNELS_TO_SCAN and keeps the uploads with at least 5x their channel's median (OUTLIER_MULTIPLE). The 10 biggest multiples go on (TOP_CANDIDATES).

First principle: an anomaly needs a cause

A 5x video means something other than the channel did the work.

The channel is already cancelled, so the cause is the topic, the packaging or luck. One video can't tell you which. So step 1 proves nothing. It produces a short list of hypotheses: "maybe this topic pulls views by itself". Step 3 is the experiment that tests them.

This is also why the list of channels matters so much. It is the only place hypotheses come from, so the results can never be more relevant to your niche than these channels are.

Step 2: the unit that repeats is the topic, not the title

Several outliers are often about the same thing, and a title is packaging. "I Built a $10k AI Agent in 6 Hours" is one creator's wrapping. The thing another creator can repeat is "building and selling AI agents". So an LLM reads the outlier titles, groups the ones about the same underlying topic, and writes for each topic the search query a viewer would type into YouTube: short, generic, no channel names, no clickbait.

Then each query goes to YouTube's own search autocomplete, which returns the phrasings viewers really type, most popular first. It costs no API quota. The LLM picks up to 3 of them (QUERY_VARIANTS): same language, still on topic, a distinct angle such as "...for beginners". Every pick is scored in step 3 as an idea of its own.

First principle: use each tool for what it is reliable at

The LLM judges language. It never produces a number.

"Are these two titles about the same thing?" is a language question, and LLMs are good at it. "How well does this topic perform?" is a measurement, and LLMs invent measurements. So every score in this system is plain arithmetic on API data, and the LLM is fenced in three ways: its answers must fit a fixed structure, its autocomplete picks are checked against the real suggestion list (an invented query is thrown away), and its answers are cached for a day, so re-running gives the same topics.

The YouTube tool  ·  step 3

Repeatability:
run the experiment.

For each query, the tool searches YouTube and asks one thing of the results: how many other channels also beat their own median with this topic?

First principle: replication

If the topic is the cause, the effect shows up wherever the topic does.

If the outlier happened because of that creator's thumbnail, or luck, other channels covering the topic will have performed normally, and the search finds no hits. If the topic itself pulls views, then channel after channel, with different thumbnails, skill and timing, will show a video that beat its baseline. Packaging and luck differ for every channel, so across many channels they cancel. The topic is the only thing they share.

What counts as a hit

hit = video_views ≥ HIT_MULTIPLE × its own channel's median (4x) AND video_views ≥ MIN_HIT_VIEWS (1,000) AND age_days ≤ MAX_HIT_AGE_DAYS (730)
RuleThe reasoning
4x its own medianThe same normalization as step 1: a hit is measured against the channel that made it.
At least 1,000 viewsOn a dead channel with a median of 4 views, a video with 17 is a "4x". A ratio means nothing when the numbers under it are noise.
At most 2 years oldViews pile up for years, but the median describes the channel today. An old video's lifetime views can't be compared with today's baseline.
Your own channel is left outYour own past hit is not independent evidence about whether it works for others.
The first 20 different channelsEvery channel examined costs quota for its baseline. CHANNELS_PER_QUERY caps the bill at the most relevant results.

First principle: count independent witnesses

Five hits from one channel are one piece of evidence. Five channels with one hit each are five.

A creator who is excellent at a topic will hit with it again and again. That tells you about the creator. So a topic only qualifies with hits from 6+ different channels (MIN_HITS). In the run on page 2, "claude code" had 22 hits across 13 channels.

The YouTube tool  ·  the score

Not every hit is
equally good evidence.

Counting hits would treat a three-week-old hit from a channel like yours the same as a two-year-old hit from a channel a thousand times your size. So each hit is weighed by how much it says about you, now.

recency = 0.5 ^ ( age_days ÷ AGE_HALF_LIFE_DAYS ) (half-life: 180 days) size_gap = | log10(their_median) − log10(your_median) | weight = recency ÷ ( 1 + size_gap ) repeatability score = sum of the weights of all the topic's hits

Recency: evidence goes stale

A hit from last month says the audience is there today. A hit from 18 months ago says it was there once. A half-life is the simplest honest model of that: every 180 days, a hit counts half as much.

Size: evidence transfers between similar channels

What works for a channel 1,000x your size may depend on an audience you don't have. The gap is measured in powers of ten, because channels differ multiplicatively: 1k to 10k views is the same kind of jump as 100k to 1M.

Their median vs yoursSize factor
The same1.00
10x bigger or smaller0.50
100x0.33
1,000x0.25

It falls slowly on purpose: a big channel's hit is weaker evidence, not worthless evidence.

Reading the score. A brand-new hit from a channel exactly your size weighs 1.0, so a score of 3.45 is "evidence worth about three and a half perfect hits". A topic qualifies with a score of 1.0+ (MIN_SCORE) and hits on 6+ channels: the channel count blocks one prolific creator, the score blocks a pile of old hits from giant channels.

Duplicates. An idea that shares more than half of its hits with a better-scoring idea (MAX_HIT_OVERLAP) is the same idea in other words, and is skipped.

The keyword tool

Rising means the floor
is rising.

Keyword Planner returns how often a keyword was searched in each of the last 48 months. The tool turns that history into one word: rising, flat, shrinking or new. Two problems have to be solved first.

Problem 1: seasons

Compare a year with a year.

"Tax software" rises every spring. Comparing this month with last month measures the calendar. So every comparison is the last 12 months against the 12 before: both windows contain every calendar month exactly once, and the seasons cancel.

Problem 2: spikes

Measure the quiet months.

One news event can double a year's average without one extra person caring a month later. So the trend comes from the floor: the mean of the 3 quietest months of a window. It is the demand that is there even when nothing is happening.

A real example: "ai agents"

Monthly Google searches for "ai agents" in the US. Blue dots are the 3 quietest months of each window, the blue line is their mean (the floor), the dotted line is the window's average.

By the average, the two years look the same: down 3%. Each contained a spike to about 90,000 searches, and spikes dominate an average. Now look at the quiet months. A year ago the slowest months had 29,467 searches. Now the slowest months have 41,033: +39%. The demand that is there when nothing special is happening has grown by more than a third. A video serves that audience, so the tool calls it rising.

The labels. rising: the floor is up 20% or more. shrinking: down 20% or more. flat: in between. new: there was no floor a year ago, and there are searches now.

The keyword tool  ·  the fine print

Know how blunt your
instrument is.

Keyword Planner does not report exact counts. It rounds every volume into buckets roughly 20 to 25% apart: 40,500, then 49,500, then 60,500. That single fact sets two thresholds.

First principle: a change smaller than your measurement error is no change

Why "rising" starts at +20%, and why tiny keywords are ignored.

When a keyword moves from one bucket to the next, the reported change is about 20% even if the true change was 2%. So anything under 20% is called flat. For the same reason, keywords under 200 searches a month (MIN_MONTHLY_SEARCHES) are dropped: down there the buckets are 10, 20, 30, and one step is a "50% rise".

The full "rising" rule in ideate.py

A keyword goes on only when all three hold:

  • its trend is rising or new (KEEP_TRENDS),
  • it has at least 200 searches a month,
  • and its searches over the whole year are not down 20% or more (YOY_FALLING_PCT).

The third check is the floor's safety net. A keyword can have a rising floor inside a collapsing year: the quiet months crept up while the total fell off a cliff. That is a topic past its peak. "ai agents" at −3% is nowhere near that line.

Keyword lookups are free. YouTube searches are not.

Connecting the two tools

YouTube and Google don't
speak the same language.

The first version of this system matched the two tools on the exact phrase. It rarely worked, and the reason is worth understanding.

On YouTube

People browse. They type long, specific, format-flavoured phrases:
"build and sell ai agent 6 hours course"

On Google

People ask. They type short noun phrases, and Keyword Planner only knows those:
"ai agent course"

Same topic, same audience, zero overlap in wording. Looked up literally, the YouTube phrase has no Google data at all, and a great topic is scored "no search volume".

First principle: judge the thing, not its label

A topic is judged as a whole, whatever phrasing each side knows it under.

On YouTube a topic is represented by its best-scoring phrasing. On Google it is represented by its most searched keyword. Deciding which phrasings belong to the same topic is a language judgement, so it is the LLM's job.

What the LLM is asked, in each direction

DirectionThe LLM's taskThe guard rail
From keywordsGroup the rising keywords into video topics. Name each topic and write its YouTube search query. Mark a topic to skip when a search would teach nothing: unrelated ("code geass"), too broad ("ai"), or a bare brand name ("chatgpt").It must copy keywords exactly. A keyword it invents is discarded, and no keyword can land in two topics.
From YouTubeFirst: write 3 short Google seed keywords for each repeatable topic. Then, from what Google suggests back: keep only the keywords of people who want the same thing as the YouTube viewers.It is told to be strict. One big, loosely related keyword would otherwise decide the whole topic's trend.

Two smaller details. Keyword Planner counts close variants ("ai agent", "ai agents") together and reports the identical 48-month history for each. The tool spots identical histories and treats them as one keyword, so searches are never counted twice. And starting from YouTube, the keyword tool is asked once per topic: in a shared request, one big topic's suggestions crowd out the rest.

Your cue to double-check. The scoreboard reports rising_keywords, such as "3 of 8". When only a few are rising, the verdict rests on the biggest keyword alone, so check that it really is the topic.

The verdict

Both tests, or no.

Every topic lands in one of four boxes. Only one of them says make it.

Rising on Google
Not rising
Repeatable on YouTube
make itProven format, growing audience.It works for channels like yours, and more people want it every month.
"repeatable, but search is shrinking"It works today, for an audience that is leaving. You would be late. The verdict names the exact reason.
Not repeatable
"rising, but not repeatable"People want it, but videos on it don't outperform. Often the demand is for an answer, a tool or a website, not for a video.
"not repeatable"Nothing here.

First principle: different failure modes need separate gates

Why it is a strict AND, not a blended score.

It is tempting to average the two numbers into one. But they guard against different mistakes, and a blend lets a huge value on one side buy forgiveness on the other. A topic with 2,000,000 searches and no repeatability is not "pretty good". It is a topic where videos don't win. Two gates can't be traded against each other.

How a topic is judged on each side

TestThe topic is judged byIt passes when
Repeatableits best-scoring phrasing on YouTubehits on MIN_HITS+ different channels and a score of MIN_SCORE+
Risingits most searched Google keywordtrend rising or new, MIN_MONTHLY_SEARCHES+ a month, whole year not down YOY_FALLING_PCT or more

Two more verdicts exist for topics that never reached YouTube: "skipped: …" with the LLM's reason, and "not searched on YouTube: outside the 10 biggest", when more topics qualified than MAX_TOPICS allows. The ones that do go are those that gained the most searches on the year before, so the quota is spent on topics that are both big and growing.

Within the scoreboard, "make it" topics come first, highest repeatability on top. Repeatability breaks the tie because it is the evidence closest to what you actually do: publish on YouTube.

Engineering

Built so you can
check its work.

A system that hands you a verdict and hides its reasoning deserves no trust. Every decision this one makes is written down where you can read it.

Design choiceWhat it gives you
One run ID everywhereA run is named by its start time, like 20260921-073628, and ideate.py hands that ID to both tools. Every file the run produced, in all three folders, carries it.
A checkpoint after every stepOutliers, topics, every scored query with its hit videos, Google's raw response, the parsed rows, the trend numbers, the reason each keyword was kept or dropped. When a verdict surprises you, you can walk back to the exact step that caused it.
The run viewerOpen index.html. Each step shows what it does, what it found in this run, and its data, down to the thumbnails of the hit videos behind a score. A run that failed shows how far it got.
A 24-hour cacheYouTube and LLM responses are stored on disk. Repeating a run costs no quota, and changing one setting re-uses everything that setting doesn't touch. --refresh pulls fresh data.
One config.pyEvery threshold in this document is a named setting with a comment. Nothing is buried in the code.

First principle: a model you can't inspect, you can't improve

Every threshold here is a judgement call. You are supposed to argue with them.

Is 4x the right bar for a hit? Is 180 days the right half-life for your niche? Nobody knows in advance. Because every step's output is saved, you can change a setting, re-run from the cache for free, and see exactly which topics moved and why.

ResourceCost

Honest limits

What it can't tell you.

First-principles thinking includes knowing where your model stops being true. These are the assumptions to keep in mind when you read a scoreboard.

LimitWhat it means for you
YouTube search shows winnersSearch ranks videos that already performed, so hits are over-represented in every result list. A score is therefore not a probability of success. Read scores against each other, across topics measured the same way.
Google search is a proxyYouTube publishes no search volumes. Google demand and YouTube demand usually move together, but not always: some topics are watched without ever being googled.
Your channel list is your horizonStarting from YouTube, the tool can only find topics that the scanned channels have already made. A topic nobody in your list has tried is invisible. Starting from keywords is the way around it.
The LLM can group wronglyIt may attach a big, loosely related keyword to a topic. That is what rising_keywords and the expandable rows in the viewer are for.
A topic is not a videoThe system cancels packaging to find topics that win regardless of it. You still have to package yours well. It tells you where to dig, not how.
Location and languageSearch data is for the United States in English by default.

The whole argument, in six lines

Normalize.
Replicate.
Weigh. Cross-check.

NormalizeDivide by the channel's median, so you compare topics and not channel sizes.
ReplicateDemand the same result from many unrelated channels, so packaging and luck cancel.
WeighTrust recent hits from channels like yours the most.
Measure the floorCompare year with year and quiet months with quiet months, so seasons and spikes cancel.
Cross-checkRequire YouTube and Google to agree, judged per topic and not per phrase.
Show your workSave every step, so every verdict can be traced and every threshold argued with.
